The lens widens · NBER Working Paper 35219

A new study measures mobility five ways, not just income.

Binder, Risch & Voorheis (2026), Lands of Opportunity, link Census, tax, and property records for 3.4 million families to estimate mobility for every U.S. county across five economic concepts.

Total income

All income: wages, business, capital, transfers. The classic measure.

Labor income

Earnings from work alone: wages and salaries.

Homeownership

Whether a child owns a home at all: the door to wealth.

Housing wealth

The value of the home owned as an adult.

Total wealth

Housing plus financial assets: stocks, retirement, business equity.

For the first time, we can ask: does wealth mobility look like income mobility?

Binder, Risch & Voorheis (2026), NBER WP 35219. Public-use county data, U.S. Census Bureau.

9 · A new study measures mobility five ways · 1:15–2:55

That has just changed. Economists Binder, Risch and Voorheis, two of them at the Census Bureau, built the largest dataset of its kind. They start with the 2000 Census long form, the survey that went to one in six households, add tax returns, and find 3.4 million teenagers living with their parents. Then they follow those children to 2019 through 2021, when they're 34 to 42.

[→ income cards] Two measures are income, from tax and W-2 records: total income, and labor income, which is wages alone.

[→ wealth cards] Three are wealth. Homeownership: does the child own a home at all? Housing wealth: what that home is worth, from property and deed records that cover every deeded home in the country. And total wealth, which adds stocks, bonds, retirement accounts and businesses. Tax records don't list those assets, so the authors estimate them from the income they pay out, like dividends and interest. One caveat: this is gross wealth, what families own, not what they owe. That's different from the net-worth figures Liz showed.

[→] Same families, same method, every county. So for the first time we can ask: does wealth mobility look like income mobility?

How to read it · three ideas, illustrated

Two numbers do the work.

αAbsolute mobility
How high do kids from the bottom reach? In Mecklenburg, they land at the 23rd income rung; the national median is 34. Higher is better.
βPersistence
How sticky is your starting position? The slope from parent's rank to child's. Higher = less mobility.
βgapWealth–income gap
Wealth β minus income β. Positive means wealth is inherited more rigidly than income.
Values for Mecklenburg County, NC. Source: Binder, Risch & Voorheis (2026).

10 · How to read it: α, β, and the gap · 2:55–4:30: core slide, slow down

Here's how that becomes a number. Everyone is ranked from 0 to 100 against their own generation, separately for each measure, and that includes renters and families with no wealth at all. Then, for each county where the children grew up, the authors fit one line: the child's rank against the parent's rank.

That line gives two numbers. [→ 1] The first is alpha, the green dot: how high do kids from the bottom actually reach? In Mecklenburg, kids who start at the bottom land at the 23rd rung on income. The national median is 34. Higher is better.

[→ 2] The second is beta, the slope. A flat line means your parents don't decide where you end up. A steep line means they do. For income, Mecklenburg's slope is 0.43.

[→ 3] Now switch to wealth. The line gets steeper: 0.51. Income is a flow you re-earn every year. Wealth, mostly a house, is a stock that compounds on its own.

[→ 4] That wedge between the two lines is what I call the wealth–income persistence gap: housing-wealth beta minus income beta, calculated from their published estimates. When it's positive, wealth is passed down more rigidly than income.

Q&A: methods backgrounder

Sources: Binder, Risch & Voorheis (2026), Lands of Opportunity, NBER WP 35219, Section 2 ("Data and Methods"); the public county file's readme; and Venkitasubramanian (2026), The Wealth–Income Mobility Gap in the Charlotte Region, "Data and Methods" and "Limitations".

Who is in the data

  • 3.4 million parent–child pairs. Children born 1978–1986, so aged 34–42 in 2020 when their adult outcomes are measured. Parents averaged 46 years old in 2000.
  • How families were linked. Inside the Census Bureau's secure environment, using anonymized person and address keys (scrambled Social Security numbers, not names).
  • The starting point is the 2000 Census long form, sent to a random 1 in 6 households (the forerunner of today's American Community Survey). Children aged 14–16 are linked to the household's reference person.
  • These links are supplemented with parent–dependent links from 1994 and 1998 tax returns (form 1040), which catch children the survey misses.
  • Coverage. Similar to tax-based mobility studies such as Chetty et al. The survey also brings in some families who did not file taxes.

How each measure is built

Measure Parents (around 2000) Children (2019–2021)
Total income Average adjusted gross income on 1999–2001 tax returns; long-form income for non-filers Same, from 2019–2021 tax and W-2 records
Labor income Wage-and-salary income, 1999–2001 (W-2 for non-filers) Wages and salaries, 2019–2021
Homeownership Owner or renter, from the 2000 Census Whether the child owns any deeded property, 2019–2021
Housing wealth Owner's own estimate of the home's market value, on the 2000 Census Value of all homes owned. It comes from property assessment and deed records covering every deeded property (vendor: Black Knight), valued by automated valuation models that use recent nearby sales. The highest value seen over 2019–2021 is used, because many in this age group buy their first home during those years.
Total wealth Housing wealth plus financial wealth Housing wealth plus financial wealth
  • Financial wealth comes from capitalized income. Tax records report the income that assets produce, not the assets themselves.
  • Dividends (stocks), interest (bonds and savings), rents, and business income are multiplied by standard capitalization factors to estimate the asset values behind them. The method follows Saez & Zucman and Piketty, Saez & Zucman; the factors come from Smith et al. and Piketty et al.
  • For children, the authors also have capital gains, partnership and S-corporation income, and retirement account balances (form 5498).
  • Gross, not net. Debts such as mortgages, student loans and credit cards are not subtracted. Negative capitalized values are set to zero. This is the key difference from the OWC net-worth figures on slides 4–6.

How mobility is estimated

  • Ranks.
  • Each person is ranked 0–100 against their own generation, separately for each measure.
  • Children are ranked against children in the same birth cohort, nationally.
  • Survey weights make the ranks representative of the population.
  • Ranks are used instead of dollars so that people with zero (renters, non-earners, no wealth) stay in the analysis. This also follows Chetty et al.
  • One line per measure. Child rank = α + β × parent rank.
  • β (relative mobility, or persistence) is the slope. Higher β means more inherited, less mobile.
  • α (absolute or upward mobility) is the average rank reached by children of parents at the bottom.
  • Renters and people with zero get special handling. Parents with no home (or no wealth, or no wages) are given a rank equal to half their group's share. For example, if 20% of parents rent, each renter is ranked 10.
  • A separate term, δ, lets their children's outcomes differ from what the line predicts. This fit the data much better, because renters are a more mixed group than low-value owners.
  • For housing wealth, α is the outcome for children of the lowest-value homeowners, and δ compares children of renters with them.
  • The Explorer's "extensive margin" view shows δ.
  • Homeownership is modeled as the probability that the child owns a home, as a function of the parent's housing rank.

From national data to county numbers

  • The childhood county. Each county's estimates describe children who grew up there (where they lived around 2000), wherever they live as adults.
  • Small-area estimation. Direct county estimates are noisy where samples are small. A Fay–Herriot model (the paper spells it "Faye-Herriot") pulls small counties' estimates toward their commuting zone, or toward a group of commuting zones of at least 500,000 people. Large counties keep estimates close to their own data.
  • Counties with fewer than 20 parent–child pairs are suppressed.
  • 3,133 counties have estimates; 3,004 have all five measures.
  • Sample sizes in our region (rounded children counts from the public file):
  • Mecklenburg about 4,100, Gaston 1,500, York 1,300.
  • Then down to Lincoln 400, Chester 350 and Anson 300.
  • The small counties lean most on the commuting-zone estimate.

What the working paper adds

  • It uses the published county estimates as released. It does not re-link records or re-estimate anything.
  • The wealth–income persistence gap = housing-wealth β − total-income β for the same county. It is a simple difference, not a parameter estimated in the NBER paper.
  • National percentiles rank each county's β against all U.S. counties with a value for that measure. Higher percentile means more persistent, so less mobile.
  • It focuses on the 14-county Charlotte region (11 NC and 3 SC counties).

Caveats to state plainly if asked

  • Descriptive, not causal. It documents patterns, not their causes.
  • A specific cohort. It describes children born 1978–1986 who grew up in the late 1990s. Young people today face different prices, lending and jobs.
  • County averages hide neighborhoods. The Opportunity Atlas shows big differences between neighborhoods within a county.
  • Gross wealth. Families with large mortgages or other debts look wealthier here than their net worth.
  • Uncertainty. The public file reports an error estimate (MSE) for every α, β and δ. The working paper does not compute one for the gap.
  • A rough check treats the two betas as independent, which probably overstates the uncertainty because they come from the same families. On that check, Mecklenburg's gap (0.071) is about three times its standard error (betas 0.434 ± 0.013 and 0.505 ± 0.021).
  • Anson's housing-wealth β has an error about three times Mecklenburg's (±0.060), so small-county gaps are much less certain.

Likely questions

  • "Is this net worth?" No. It is gross wealth: what families own, with no debts subtracted. Liz's OWC figures are modeled net worth, a different measure.
  • "Where do the home values come from?" For children, from property and deed records, valued by automated models based on nearby sales. For parents, from the owner's own estimate on the 2000 Census.
  • "Does 'Mecklenburg' mean people living here now?" No. It means children who grew up in Mecklenburg around 2000, wherever they live now.
  • "Can you break it down by race?" Not with the public county file. It reports all children together. The NBER paper does relate county results to local characteristics, including the share of Black residents. Liz's OWC profile is where the race comparisons come from.
  • "How reliable are the small counties?" Less reliable. Their estimates are pulled toward the surrounding commuting zone, and their error estimates are larger.
  • "Why ranks and not dollars?" Ranks keep people with zero (renters, no wealth) in the analysis, make the two generations comparable, and match the standard income-mobility method.
  • "Does this describe kids today?" Not directly. It describes children born 1978–1986, measured in their mid-thirties to early forties. Prices, lending and jobs have changed since then.
